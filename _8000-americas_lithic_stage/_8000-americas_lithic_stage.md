# Introduction
A long-standing model explains presence of humans in America by existence of land bridge (Beringia) in place of modern Bering Strait. Due to globally lower temperatures and sea levels being lower by 60-120 meters than currently, it existed at least during part of the late Pleistocene (between around 45 000 - 13 500 BCE). It was frequented by groups of hunter-gatherers preying on Pleistocene megafauna. Ancestral Native American population lineage is consistent with populations deriving from East Asian groups, including additional Ancient North Eurasian ancestry, and arriving from Eastern Siberia.
After Last Glacial Maximum temperatures started to rise which enabled populations to expand further into new lands. Routes taken during this expansion and timing are subject to debate. One theory underlines importance of appearing ice-free corridors that stretched between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets (inland route). Another theory proposes route of migration down the Pacific coast, either on foot or on boats. Evidence for the coastal route is indirect as coastal areas of this time are now covered by the sea level rise but could explain movement of peoples even before thawing of ice sheets[^migration_into_americas].
For many years, the earliest human occupation of the Americas was associated with Clovis culture (known as Clovis First theory), a Paleoindian stone tool tradition that was characterised by fluted lanceolate projectile points which appeared around 11 500 BCE[^clovis_culture]. Since at least 1990s this model is being challenged by significant discoveries dated around 12 000 BCE and older, though firm dates are difficult to obtain. This shifts narrative from a single migration event to possibility of multiple small, diverse groups entering the continent at various points in time[^pre-clovis_americas].
